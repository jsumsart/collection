About

The collection and its digital record

The Jackson State University Department of Art Permanent Collection reflects decades of teaching, artistic production, exhibition, collecting, and preservation at Jackson State University. Formed through the everyday work of the Department of Art, the collection documents the creative lives of faculty, students, alumni, visiting artists, and the broader cultural communities connected to the university.

How The Collection Began

The collection developed over the working life of Jackson State University's Department of Art rather than through a single formal museum acquisition program. Works entered university care through classrooms and studios, faculty and student exhibitions, visiting-artist programs, workshops, gifts, teaching purchases, and long-term acts of preservation.

Over time, these objects became a record of the department's artistic and educational history. Early faculty leadership, including Lawrence Jones and Maria Jones, helped establish a visual culture at Jackson State grounded in teaching, artistic practice, mentorship, and engagement with the wider community.

The collection consequently preserves more than individual works of art. It provides evidence of the people, relationships, exhibitions, educational practices, and cultural networks that shaped art at Jackson State University across generations.

Mission

The mission of the Jackson State University Department of Art Permanent Collection is to preserve, document, research, and share the artistic history of the department and the communities with which it has been connected.

The collection supports teaching and learning, creates opportunities for original research, expands public access to works held by the university, and preserves the historical record of artists, faculty, students, alumni, exhibitions, and cultural networks associated with Jackson State University.

Digitization is an important part of this mission. By making collection information more accessible, the database creates new opportunities to study individual objects while also tracing connections among artists, places, exhibitions, institutions, and histories.

Digitization And Database Development

The digitization of the collection and development of this database were led by Dr. Brittany Myburgh and Dr. Detrice Roberts, with support from the Africana Digital Humanities Lab at Jackson State University.

The project brings together collections research, digital humanities methodologies, archival investigation, object documentation, and database development to create a sustainable digital record of the Department of Art Permanent Collection.

The database is intended not simply as an online catalog, but as an evolving scholarly resource. As research continues, records may be expanded or revised to incorporate newly identified artists, exhibition histories, provenance information, archival materials, and other documentation.

How To Read The Records

The records presented on this site draw from the active collection catalog, direct study of objects, departmental records, exhibition pamphlets and catalogs, newspaper research, archival sources, oral histories, artist interviews, and the shared knowledge of faculty, alumni, former students, and community members.

Because the collection developed over many decades, documentation varies considerably from object to object. Some records are therefore more complete than others. Dates, titles, artist identifications, exhibition histories, and provenance information may be updated as additional research becomes available.

Rather than concealing those gaps, the database makes the ongoing nature of collections research visible. Revision, attribution, and the recovery of missing information are part of the work of preserving the collection responsibly.

Institutional And Cultural Reach

The collection connects Jackson State University to a much larger history of Black artistic production and education in Mississippi and beyond.

Its records reveal relationships extending from the university to Johnson Hall, Farish Street, Lynch Street, Tougaloo College, Memphis, the Mississippi Delta, and wider networks of artists, educators, institutions, and communities. These connections demonstrate how faculty and students at Jackson State built an expansive visual culture through teaching, mentorship, exhibition, travel, collaboration, and sustained community engagement.

Seen collectively, the works preserve an important record of how artistic communities were created and maintained, often through resourcefulness, personal relationships, institutional commitment, and long-term acts of care.
